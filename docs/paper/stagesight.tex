\begin{abstract}
A river gauge reports stage from a single pressure sensor. When that sensor drifts, freezes or
goes silent, often during the very flood it is meant to record, the record shows no sign of it.
Many USGS gauges already carry a camera aimed at a staff plate, but its images are inspected by
people, not measured. StageSight turns that camera into an independent second instrument.
Classical OpenCV~5 operations and the OpenCV DNN text recognizer read the stage from the plate's
printed marks and numbers, \emph{without any gauge data}. On the 29~July 2026 flood at Hancock,
NY (USGS 01421500; NWS minor flood, 18.08~ft peak), the camera agrees with the USGS gauge to
0.26~ft RMSE (95\% bootstrap interval 0.20--0.31~ft; 29 of 30 readings within 0.5~ft), whereas a
regression trained on a smaller September high water has 6.3~ft RMSE. An event-gated agent (Claude on Amazon
Bedrock) compares camera and gauge, gathers evidence with tools, and queues sensor flags and
flood alerts for human approval. \AgentAbstract{} The system runs live on AWS Lambda (Graviton), EventBridge, S3, SNS and
Bedrock, and unchanged on a laptop CPU; on Graviton4 the Cloud-Optimized OpenCV build (COOL)
gives output identical to stock OpenCV.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Stage, the water level at a gauge, drives flood forecasts, warnings and discharge records. A
gauge measures it with one sensor, here a pressure transducer, and that sensor fails in quiet
ways: it fouls with sediment, its line freezes, its telemetry drops out. Hydrographers find
these faults later, during record review; forecasters and emergency managers may act on the bad
value in real time.

The USGS operates cameras at many of its gauges and serves their images through the NIMS
API~\cite{nims}. Published camera methods measure stage from such images with deep
segmentation and photogrammetry~\cite{eltner2021,blanch2026}, learned
regression~\cite{vandaele2021,xu2025}, or purpose-built staff-gauge and stage-camera
systems~\cite{sabbatini2021,noto2022}. They need surveyed control points, dedicated cameras, or
training labels from the very gauge one would want to check. We found none that uses the camera
to \emph{audit} the gauge.

\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item A gauge-free reader for existing staff-plate cameras: the plate's own marks give a 1-D
projective pixel-to-feet map, the printed foot numbers (read with \cv{dnn.TextRecognitionModel})
give the datum, and per-mark color contrast gives the waterline. It reads a flood higher than any
water level seen in setup at 0.26~ft RMSE, and it returns a status instead of a guess when it cannot see.
\item An event-gated agent that arbitrates between two instruments: code detects disagreement,
silence, blindness and flood levels; only then does Claude gather evidence (earlier frames, gauge
history, an upstream gauge) and act. A rule planner with the same tools is the baseline.
\item A safe operating design, deployed serverless on AWS Graviton: agent memory, a safety net
that always surfaces flood alerts, a rule fallback and a human approval queue, evaluated with
repeated replays of injected gauge and camera faults on a real flood, failures included.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
Eltner et al.~\cite{eltner2021} segment water with a CNN and intersect the waterline with a
photogrammetric 3-D model; Blanch et al.~\cite{blanch2026} operate the same family for years at
dedicated stations with surveyed ground control. Vandaele et al.~\cite{vandaele2021}
transfer-learn water segmentation on public river cameras but stop at segmentation. Xu and
Wang~\cite{xu2025} assess deep networks that regress gauge height from webcam images; such
models learn from the gauge record and are bounded by its range. Staff-gauge vision
systems~\cite{sabbatini2021} and low-cost stage cameras~\cite{noto2022} read water level at
sites built for the purpose. StageSight reads an absolute stage from the plate of an existing
public camera, needs neither survey nor training labels, and is designed to disagree with the
gauge when the gauge is wrong.

\section{Data}
\textbf{Camera.} The NIMS ``Hancock Staff'' camera at USGS 01421500, 1080$\times$1920 frames,
one per hour, 21~July to 19~October 2026 (1,869 frames). The camera started on 14~April 2026;
the 29~July event is its only flood. \textbf{Gauge.} USGS gage height (parameter 00065, 15-min),
used only to evaluate the reader and, by the agent, as the instrument under test.
\textbf{Upstream.} USGS 01417500 at Harvard, NY (an agent tool; cross-correlation over 90 days
gives a lag of about 3~h). \textbf{Flood levels.} NWS NWPS, HKCN6: action 14~ft, minor flood
18~ft. All data are public.

\subsection{Setup, once per camera}
\textbf{Plate and marks.} Red pixels are found by an HSV threshold and a $3\times3$ opening;
\cv{fitLine} with a Huber loss gives the plate axis $\mathbf{d}$ through origin $\mathbf{o}$.
Each connected red component is a mark. The long foot marks (area $\geq 0.6\times$ the median of
the eight largest) are projected onto the axis, $t_i=(\mathbf{c}_i-\mathbf{o})^\top\mathbf{d}$;
a gap of about twice the local spacing is indexed as a missing mark.

\textbf{Scale.} A pinhole camera maps points on a straight plate to the image line by a 1-D
homography, so plate units $u$ (ft below the top foot mark) follow
\begin{equation}
u(t)=\frac{a\,t+b}{c\,t+1},\qquad
\begin{bmatrix} t_i & 1 & -t_i u_i\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} a\\ b\\ c\end{bmatrix}=u_i ,
\label{eq:homography}
\end{equation}
solved by linear least squares over the foot marks (fit error 0.05~ft). Three parameters fixed
by geometry extrapolate along the plate where a free polynomial would not.

\textbf{Datum.} The plate is rotated upright (\cv{warpAffine}), red is whitened, and the black
digits beside each foot mark are cropped. The OpenCV Zoo CRNN~\cite{shi2015crnn,zoo} runs through
\cv{dnn.TextRecognitionModel} with CTC greedy decoding. In a frame, each read $k$ at mark $i$
proposes the datum $D=k+u_i$; a dropped leading ``1'' is the usual misread, so only the longest
reads may vote. Across the setup frames the majority wins: $D=20$~ft in 6 of 7 frames (\texttt{sites/hancock/site.json}). Stage is
then $s=D-u$.

\subsection{Every frame}
\textbf{Quality gate.} Frames that are blurred (variance of the Laplacian below 40), dark or
over-exposed are \texttt{rejected}; frames of another size are \texttt{wrong\_size}.
\textbf{Registration.} The camera moves a few pixels between days. \cv{phaseCorrelate} on the
always-dry top of the plate (Hann-windowed) gives the shift; above 80~px the status is
\texttt{camera\_moved}.
\textbf{Mark visibility.} For each of the $n=29$ red components (foot and half-foot marks and
their fragments), the median Lab $a^*$ inside it is compared with a ring of non-red pixels
around it (\cv{dilate}, $15\times15$ minus $5\times5$):
\begin{equation}
v_j=\mathbf{1}\!\left[\operatorname{med}_{\text{mark }j}a^*-\operatorname{med}_{\text{ring }j}a^*\geq 8\right].
\label{eq:vis}
\end{equation}
The test is relative, so it holds in sun, shade and overcast; water and foam over a mark remove
its contrast.
\textbf{Waterline.} With marks ordered top to bottom, the split
\begin{equation}
k^\star=\arg\min_k\;\textstyle\sum_{j<k}(1-v_j)+\sum_{j\geq k}v_j
\end{equation}
tolerates a few wrong marks (glare, a leaf). The waterline $t^\star$ is the midpoint of the gap
between mark $k^\star\!-\!1$ and mark $k^\star$, and $s=D-u(t^\star)$. Consecutive possible
waterlines are 0.08--0.79~ft apart, which sets the resolution of one reading.
\textbf{Status.} $k^\star\geq n-1$ (all marks visible, or only the lowest hidden, which is
ambiguous at low water) gives \texttt{below\_plate}; $k^\star=0$ gives \texttt{unreadable}
(night infrared, fog, spray). The reader never extrapolates below the plate.

\section{The Agent}
OpenCV runs on every frame; Claude is called only when code raises an event
(Table~\ref{tab:events}). The planner receives the event, the open issues and its own recent
actions (memory), and runs a manual tool loop with adaptive thinking through the Anthropic
Bedrock client. Evidence tools: \texttt{reread\_earlier\_frames} (re-runs the reader, with mark
counts), \texttt{get\_site\_gauge}, \texttt{get\_upstream\_gauge}. Final actions:
\texttt{flag\_sensor} and \texttt{draft\_alert} (both enter the approval queue),
\texttt{publish\_provisional\_stage} and \texttt{no\_action}. Two operator facts are in the
prompt: readings move in steps of about 0.5~ft, and upstream changes arrive about 3~h later. The
prompt also gives the reader's accuracy, 0.26~ft RMSE, which was measured on the same flood.

\textbf{Safety design.} (i) \emph{Safety net}: a \texttt{level\_rise} always yields an alert
draft for the operator; if the agent doubted it, its doubt is written into the draft.
(ii) \emph{Fallback}: on any API or connection error, a refusal, or no final action, the rule
planner decides and the log says so. (iii) \emph{Human in the loop}: no flag or alert leaves the
system without an operator's approval.

\section{System on AWS}
Both functions run on arm64 (Graviton) from one zip package that bundles the Linux arm64 wheels
of \texttt{opencv-python-headless} 5.0.0.93 and the flood replay; it is built without Docker and
deployed by an idempotent script that creates the bucket, the IAM role, both functions, the
schedule, a Function URL and an SNS topic for approved alerts (Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}). The role can
write logs, read and write only the project bucket, invoke Anthropic models on Bedrock and publish
to that one topic. From CloudWatch, a scheduled tick (download,
read, event check, write) takes a median of 6.3~s ($n=23$, peak memory 295~MB) and an API
request a median of 47~ms ($n=1{,}227$). Claude is called only on events: none on a quiet day,
two or three during the flood, each with three evidence calls and one to three actions. With
Opus~4.6 one replayed scenario used \AgentTokens{} (median of three runs; adaptive thinking,
effort medium).

\textbf{COOL on Graviton4.} The live tick runs on Lambda; a new site needs a batch pass over its
camera archive, and that pass runs on EC2 Graviton4 with the Cloud-Optimized OpenCV Library (COOL~3.1:
OpenCV 5.1-dev with KleidiCV~0.7.0, built for Neoverse-V2). We measured it against the pip
\texttt{opencv-python-headless}~5.0.0 that Lambda uses, on the same instance (Table~\ref{tab:cool};
\texttt{scripts/cool\_run.py} launches the instance, runs both builds, uploads the results and
terminates it). Both builds read the same status and stage in every frame. With 16 worker processes a
c8g.4xlarge reads the whole 90-day archive (\BatchFrames{} frames) in \BatchSeconds~s, about
\$\CostArchive{} at on-demand prices (\$\CostPerK{} per 1,000 frames); throughput scales almost
linearly from \BatchOne{} frames/s with one worker. COOL is 4\% faster in a single process and up to
4.9$\times$ faster in single kernels, but the batch is bound by NumPy and Python, so both builds tie
there. COOL's DNN text recognizer aborted on c8g.xlarge in both recorded runs (no OCR result in either)
and ran normally on c8g.4xlarge; a minimal reproduction is in \texttt{scripts/cool\_dnn\_repro.py}.

\section{Experiments}
\subsection{Accuracy on a real flood}
Test frames are the daylight frames of 21~July--1~August with the water on the plate ($n=30$,
29--31~July). The baselines are trained on the 15 frames of the 4--5~September high water
(up to 11.97~ft); 27 of the 30 test frames lie above that range.

As Table~\ref{tab:accuracy} and Fig.~\ref{fig:flood} show, StageSight is the only gauge-free
method and the most accurate. Fitting the offset to the gauge is worse (0.37~ft): the lowest
mark is often wet at low water, which biases the fit. The linear baseline fails because the
flood lies outside its training range. Two flood frames are regression tests in the repository
(17.77 vs 17.61~ft; 12.63 vs 12.47~ft). The 30 test frames are hourly and correlated, so the
bootstrap interval is optimistic; they come from one flood at one camera.

\textbf{Ablations.} A sub-mark edge search inside the bracket gave 0.275~ft RMSE against
0.257~ft for the midpoint and was not adopted. Using the bracket between two marks as an
uncertainty interval covered the gauge in only 23--50\% of frames, so we report no intervals.

obscured. The plate is a flood instrument: it reads when the river is high, which is when a
gauge fault matters most.

\subsection{Agent: injected faults on the real flood}
The replay feeds the 29~July flood frame by frame and injects four gauge faults and two camera
faults; the camera faults are synthetic overlays on real frames, placed by us. A fault counts
as caught when the gauge is flagged (or, for a silent gauge, the camera stage is published)
within the fault window plus 2~h; a camera fault counts as handled when the camera is blamed
and the healthy gauge is not. The rule planner uses the same tools with fixed thresholds.
Each Claude model ran every scenario \AgentRuns{} times (Table~\ref{tab:agent}).

\subsection{Development history of the agent}
\label{sec:history}
We report every Claude run. \emph{Run 1} (no saved log) missed the drift and blamed the camera;
the two operator facts above were added. \emph{Run 2} caught every fault but, in the frozen-gauge
case, forgot its own gauge flag and drafted no flood alert during a real flood. The fix was in
code, not in the prompt: memory of open issues and the safety net. \emph{Run 3}, the single run
shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:timeline}: the safety net fired once, again in the frozen-gauge case.
Claude flagged the frozen gauge, but partly for a wrong reason: it inferred from the 3~h lag
that the river was receding, while this flood peaked about 7~h after the upstream peak. It
therefore dismissed the 18.44~ft flood-level camera reading at 17:00 (true 18.08~ft) and flagged
the camera, and its provisional stages were 0.3--0.45~ft low. We did not re-tune the lag to this flood. The repeated runs in
Table~\ref{tab:agent} use the code of run~3.

\section{Limitations and Threats to Validity}
\textbf{One flood, one camera.} The accuracy rests on 30 correlated frames of one event, and the
agent results on replays of that event; the camera faults are synthetic. \textbf{Ground truth.}
The USGS gauge is the reference, although it is the instrument the system questions.
\textbf{Night}: infrared frames show the plate but not the red marks (\texttt{unreadable}).
\textbf{Low water}: below about 11~ft the plate is dry and the camera cannot check the gauge.
\textbf{Plate designs}: other NIMS plates (Owasco, Moravia, Chenango, NY) use black marks or
0.1~ft text, or stand on dry bank; they are not supported yet. \textbf{No plate}: a
self-calibrated research mode learns a per-pixel flood-stage map from the archive (0.29~ft MAE in
range on the Hancock wall) but does not extrapolate to floods; on far-bank bridge cameras
(Hermann, MO; De Soto, KS; 2--3~px per ft) it reaches only 3.4 and 2.3~ft RMSE in range.
\textbf{Models}: Claude Opus~4.6 and Sonnet~4.6 are the Claude models available on this AWS
account; the model is a configuration setting (\texttt{AGENT\_MODEL}).

\section{Responsible Operation}
StageSight is a decision aid, not an official warning system. Nothing leaves the system until an
operator approves it; approved flags and alerts are then e-mailed through Amazon SNS. Live
approvals require an operator token, and approvals in the public
replay change nothing. A camera stage that stands in for the gauge is labeled provisional. The
system uses public data and no personal data. The bucket is private, the role is limited as
above, failed ticks are not retried, and every agent decision, tool call and reason is logged
and shown on the dashboard. The public \texttt{/measure} endpoint accepts only frames of this camera's size (1080$\times$1920).

\section{Conclusion}
A camera that already watches a staff plate can measure stage by geometry and OCR alone, through
a flood above everything seen in setup, and that independent reading lets an agent tell which
instrument is wrong. Next: read night infrared frames from the printed numbers and support more
plate designs across the NIMS network.
